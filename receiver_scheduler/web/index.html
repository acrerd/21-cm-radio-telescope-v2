Bandpass template
Everything from the feed to the spectrum shapes the band — the SAWbird’s filter, the amplifier after it, the AD9361’s decimation chain and the FPGA’s down-converter. Measuring the whole product at once means pointing at sky with no hydrogen in it — the Lockman Hole, l=150 b=+53, peaks at only 1.3 K. The line itself is masked and the fit bridges it, so “empty” is only assumed where it is true.

It measures wherever the dish is already pointing, and does not slew. A template has to match the elevation and the hour of whatever it will reduce — two taken 4.7 hours apart differed by 2% — so moving to a fixed field would guarantee the wrong one. A direction carrying more than 1.5 K of hydrogen outside the masked window is refused: that is emission a template would fit as instrument response and then subtract from every observation.

Gain and system temperature
With the band flat, counts still are not kelvin. The simulator gives the antenna temperature any direction should show through this beam, so a straight line through counts against kelvin gives the gain as its slope and the system temperature from its intercept. The pointing is chosen now, not named: the gain drifts, and a fixed source is below the horizon for most of the day. It searches the sky rather than the plane alone — hydrogen does not stop at b = 0, and when the plane has set there is usually still a 70 K peak a few degrees off it, higher in the sky than the plane ever was.
Suggestions for right now, brightest first and spread at least 25° apart. A direction clearing by only a degree or two is worth a look outside before you trust it, since the trees grow between scans.

Horizon Scan

Maps the skyline by radiometry: trees, roofs and the dome tower are all near 290 K at 1420 MHz, so lowering the beam through them is a large step in total power. Run it at night, dry and calm — the Sun would swamp the sky level and wet or moving foliage changes what is measured.

Solar flux, live
Band power from the record the receiver has just written, turned into flux with the stored gain and the system temperature subtracted, so what is plotted is the Sun alone, then divided by the atmospheric transmission at the Sun's elevation so the number is the flux above the atmosphere — which is what a published index quotes. All of that is display only; the recording keeps what the receiver measured. Read from a summary the receiver writes beside its file — the recording itself cannot be opened while it is being written. The full spectra are recorded as usual and can be reduced properly afterwards.

The time axis is the observation's own window — start to stop, fixed from the moment it begins — so the scan fills in from the left and the dashed line marks the crossing time the pointing was laid out for. A source should peak there.

The number is the median across the band, which is the continuum level: it follows a broadband source transiting the beam, and is deliberately insensitive to narrow interference and to the LO artefact. That also makes it insensitive to a spectral line covering a small part of the band, so an H I drift scan may show little here while recording the line perfectly well. Maps the obstructed horizon by radiometry, one altitude cut per azimuth. Schedule it for a dark, dry, calm night: the Sun swamps the sky level, and wet or wind-blown foliage is not the horizon you want recorded. Allow about two hours.

The dish parks where the source will be at the beam-crossing time and stays fixed while the sky drifts through the beam. Recording runs from T−window to T+window; start time and duration are derived automatically.
