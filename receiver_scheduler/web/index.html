Getting started

This is a 3 m dish radio telescope tuned to the 21 cm line of neutral hydrogen. It maps hydrogen gas through the Galaxy and measures how fast that gas moves. It works by day and through cloud, because radio waves pass through the atmosphere and the Sun does not blind a radio receiver.

What the telescope is made of

  • A dish on an alt–azimuth mount: it points by swinging in azimuth (compass bearing) and altitude (angle above the horizon).
  • A receiver (a software-defined radio) that records the radio spectrum around 1420 MHz.
  • Two web pages: this Scheduler, where you plan and run observations and look at the data, and the Controller (the Open Controller button at the top of the page), which drives the mount directly.

The status bar

The strip at the top of every tab always tells you the state of the system: whether an observation is running, where the telescope is pointing and whether it is slewing or homed, and whether the receiver is busy. Glance at it before you start anything.

Ground rules

Be gentle with the mountThe dish is a real, wearing machine. It has hard limits in both axes and homes itself against them. Avoid needless slewing, and never assume it has finished moving — watch the status bar. If something looks wrong, the red Stop button halts the current run.
Turn on Help tipsThe Help tips switch at the top right shows a one-line explanation whenever you hover over a control. Leave it on while you are learning.

When you are ready, read Your first observation.

Your first observation

A good first target is the galactic plane: it is full of hydrogen, so the line is strong and unmistakable. Here is the whole loop, start to finish.

  1. Check the telescope is ready. In the status bar, the Telescope indicator should say it is homed and idle, not faulted. If it has never been homed today, home it first (the schedule form has a Home first option).
  2. See what is up. Open the Simulator. It shows the sky over the observatory now, the measured horizon, and where the bright sources and the galactic plane are. Pick a point on the plane that is well above the horizon.
  3. Book it. On the Scheduler tab, add an observation: choose the target, a start time, a duration (a few minutes is plenty for the plane), and whether to track it or take a drift scan (see Tracked vs drift scan). Save it.
  4. Let it run. At the start time the telescope slews, settles, and records. The status bar shows the running observation and offers Stop. It writes an HDF5 file you can open while it is still being written.
  5. Look at the data. Open the Observe tab and view the recording. You will see a spectrum with a peak near the hydrogen line. Read it on the velocity axis — see Reading a spectrum.
What success looks likeA clear bump rising out of the noise, within a few hundred km/s of zero. That bump is hydrogen gas in the Milky Way, Doppler-shifted by the Galaxy's rotation.

Scheduling observations

The Scheduler tab is the diary of the telescope. Each entry says what to observe, when, for how long, and how. At the start time the scheduler slews the dish, waits for it to settle, runs the receiver, and stows or homes afterwards if you asked it to.

What goes in an entry

  • Target. A named object (the Sun, the Moon), a fixed alt/az direction, an RA/Dec position, or galactic coordinates l, b.
  • Time and duration. When to start and how long to record. Longer means less noise (see Temperature & noise) but more mount time.
  • Mode. Track the source across the sky, or park and let it drift through the beam — Tracked vs drift scan.
  • Respect local horizon. Leave this on: it keeps the dish from observing through the trees and dome towers, which are warm and would contaminate the data.
One telescope, one userOnly one thing can hold the dish and receiver at a time: an observation, a Sun scan, a calibration, a horizon scan. A scheduled observation takes priority and will interrupt a calibration to run on time.
The tuning is fixedEvery scheduled observation records with the same receiver setup (the “fixed instrument”), chosen so the hydrogen line always lands in a clean part of the band. You do not set the frequency per observation; it is shown for reference only.

Tracked vs drift scan

The most important choice in an observation is whether the dish follows the source or stays still. It decides what successive rows of the data mean.

Tracked observation

The mount continuously re-points to keep the source in the beam as the Earth turns. Every spectrum in the file looks at the same piece of sky, so you can add them up to beat down the noise and study one direction deeply. Use it to measure the hydrogen profile of a chosen point.

Drift scan

The mount is parked at a fixed spot and the sky drifts through the beam as the Earth rotates (about 15° per hour, so a 5° beam takes ~20 minutes to cross). Successive spectra look at different sky: the source rises in signal as it enters the beam, peaks at the centre, and falls as it leaves. Use it to measure a source's brightness and the beam's shape, with the mount barely moving.

Why the distinction is recordedThe file stores track or drift as the observation mode, because it is the difference between “these rows are the same sky” and “these rows are a cut across the sky.” An alt/az “fixed direction” entry is a drift scan.

Reading a spectrum

Open a recording from the Observe tab. The plot is power against velocity (or frequency). The hydrogen line appears as one or more bumps above a flat baseline.

The axes

  • Horizontal: the recorded axis is frequency, but it is shown as a velocity in km/s, because that is what tells you about the gas. Zero velocity is the rest frequency of the line, 1420.405751 MHz.
  • Vertical: received power. Once the telescope is calibrated this is an antenna temperature in kelvin (see Temperature & noise); before that it is raw counts.

Which velocity frame

The plot uses the LSR (Local Standard of Rest) frame whenever the direction and time are known, so your measurement can be compared with published data and with the same source months later. The raw file stays in the telescope's own (topocentric) frame; the conversion is done only for display. The plot labels which frame it is in — details in Doppler & velocity frames.

Making sense of the shape

Along a line of sight through the Galaxy you look through gas at many distances, each moving at a different speed, so the line is a blend of contributions at different velocities. Multiple peaks mean multiple clouds or spiral arms along the way. The velocity of a peak, through the Galaxy's rotation, encodes where that gas is — see Galactic rotation.

Sun scan & calibration

Two things must be true before your numbers mean anything: the dish must point where it says it does, and received counts must convert to a real temperature. Both are settled by looking at the Sun, which is a strong, well-known radio source.

Pointing

On the Sun Scan tab the telescope rasters a small grid around the Sun and finds where the signal actually peaks. The offset between that and where it aimed is a pointing error. Collect these over a range of Sun positions and fit a pointing model that corrects the mount everywhere — see Pointing & calibration.

Temperature scale

The same measurement gives the gain (counts per kelvin) and the system temperature Tsys, which together put spectra on a kelvin scale. Because the receiver cools through the evening the gain drifts a little, so calibrate close in time to the observation that needs absolute temperatures.

Why the Sun, and not hydrogenCalibrating a hydrogen measurement against a hydrogen model would be circular. The Sun is an independent, broadband source, so it is an honest ruler for both pointing and temperature.

Simulator & horizon

The Simulator

The Simulator is a planetarium for the telescope. It draws the sky over the observatory in real time: the horizon, the galactic plane, the Sun and Moon, and the bright radio sources. Use it to see what is observable now, to understand what a target will do during your observation, and to picture what the beam is sweeping up.

The measured horizon

Trees, the roofline and the dome towers are warm at 1420 MHz — foliage glows at roughly room temperature in the radio — so pointing the beam through them injects a large false signal. The Horizon tab maps this obstructed skyline radiometrically. The orange line on the Simulator is that measurement, and it is the rule that decides whether a target is high enough to observe cleanly.

Rule of thumbIf a target sits below the orange horizon line in the Simulator, the trees will be in the beam. Wait for it to rise, or pick another target.

The 21 cm line

Neutral hydrogen atoms — a single proton and electron — emit a faint radio line at a precise frequency. It is the single most useful spectral line in radio astronomy, and it is what this telescope is built to detect.

Where it comes from

In the ground state of hydrogen the electron and proton spins can be parallel (slightly higher energy) or antiparallel (slightly lower). When an atom flips from parallel to antiparallel it releases the tiny energy difference as a photon:

ν0 = 1420.405751 MHz,   λ = c/ν0 ≈ 21.1 cm,   E = hν0 ≈ 5.9 μeV

This is a forbidden transition: a single atom waits, on average, about 11 million years to flip on its own. The line is bright only because the Galaxy contains an enormous amount of hydrogen — there are so many atoms along any line of sight that a steady trickle of photons adds up to an easily detectable signal.

What it tells you

Where the gas is optically thin (usually the case), the brightness of the line is proportional to how many hydrogen atoms lie along the line of sight — the column density:

NH ≈ 1.82×1018 ∫ TB(v) dv   [cm−2, with TB in K and v in km/s]

So the strength of the line measures how much gas there is, and its Doppler shift measures how fast that gas is moving — which, through the rotation of the Galaxy, reveals where it is. Those two facts are the whole basis of 21 cm astronomy.

Why 1420 MHz is protectedThis frequency is reserved for radio astronomy by international agreement, so terrestrial transmitters may not sit on the hydrogen line.

Doppler & velocity frames

The line is emitted at a fixed frequency, so any shift you observe is caused by motion along the line of sight. Reading it correctly means knowing what the telescope's own motion contributes.

The Doppler shift

Gas moving away from you stretches the wave to a lower frequency (redshift); gas moving toward you compresses it to a higher frequency (blueshift). For the speeds involved here (≪ c) the radial velocity is

vr / c = (ν0 − ν) / ν0 = Δλ / λ
Positive vr means the gas is receding (lower observed frequency). Converting the frequency axis this way gives the velocity axis you read on a spectrum.

Why the frame matters

Your telescope is not a fixed platform. It spins with the Earth (~0.5 km/s), orbits the Sun (~30 km/s), and the Sun itself moves. All of that is folded into the raw (topocentric) velocity, up to about ±30 km/s depending on the direction and the date. Observe the same cloud six months apart and the raw peak would sit at two different velocities — useless for comparison.

The Local Standard of Rest

The fix is to refer everything to the LSR: the average motion of stars in the Sun's neighbourhood, a steady local reference. The software computes the projection of the observer's motion toward the target at the observation's own time and subtracts it, so the plotted axis is what an observer at rest in the LSR would measure. The raw recording is left untouched; only the display is converted, and it is labelled with the frame it is in.

The anticentre checkToward the galactic anticentre (l = 180°) rotation contributes no radial velocity, so the line should sit near 0 km/s in the LSR. A measurement here landed at +3.75 km/s against a survey value of +3.97 — agreement to 0.2 km/s, from 15 km/s before the frame correction.

Galactic rotation

The Milky Way rotates, and it does not turn as a rigid wheel: gas at different radii moves at different speeds. That differential rotation turns a velocity measurement into a map.

Radial velocity along a sight line

Look into the galactic plane at longitude l. Gas at galactocentric radius R, orbiting at speed V(R), has a line-of-sight velocity relative to the LSR of

vr = R0 ( V(R)/R − V0/R0 ) sin l
R0 ≈ 8.2 kpc is the Sun's distance from the centre and V0 ≈ 235 km/s its orbital speed. The sin l is why gas toward the centre (l=0) and anticentre (l=180°) shows little shift, while gas at l=90° shows the most.

The tangent-point method

Inside the Sun's orbit, along one sight line, the largest radial velocity comes from the tangent point, where the line of sight is exactly along the gas's motion and R = R0 sin l. Reading the maximum velocity of the line at each longitude therefore samples the rotation curve V(R), longitude by longitude.

Where dark matter shows upThe rotation curve stays roughly flat (~235 km/s) far beyond where the visible mass thins out. Newtonian gravity from the stars alone predicts it should fall. The gap is one of the classic pieces of evidence for dark matter, and it shows in data from this telescope.

The beam & resolution

A dish does not see a point — it sees a cone of sky. How wide that cone is sets what you can and cannot tell apart.

Angular resolution

Diffraction blurs any aperture. For a dish of diameter D observing wavelength λ, the beam width is about

θ ≈ λ / D  (radians)  =  0.211 m / 3 m ≈ 0.07 rad ≈ 4°
In practice this dish's beam is measured at about 5° across (full width at half maximum). That is roughly ten times the width of the full Moon.

What that means for you

  • The telescope cannot resolve individual clouds; it averages everything within ~5° into one measurement.
  • A source smaller than the beam (like the Sun, half a degree across) is smeared out to the beam's width — that smearing is the beam shape, which a drift scan measures.
  • To point and calibrate well you must know the beam, because it sets how much of a source's signal you actually collect.
A wide beam is not a handicap hereA wide beam is poor for imaging but good for surveying diffuse gas. The Milky Way's hydrogen fills the sky on scales far larger than 5°, so a dish this size maps it well.

Temperature & noise

Radio astronomers measure power as a temperature. Two equations set what you can detect, and how long it takes.

Antenna temperature

In the radio, the power a receiver collects in a bandwidth Δν is proportional to a temperature (the Rayleigh–Jeans law), so signals are quoted in kelvin:

P = kB T Δν

The telescope always measures the sum of the sky signal and the receiver's own noise. The receiver noise is captured by the system temperature Tsys (a few hundred kelvin for this instrument); the source adds an antenna temperature TA on top.

The radiometer equation

You cannot measure TA perfectly, because the total power fluctuates. The noise on a measurement falls as you average more:

ΔT = Tsys / √( Δν · τ )
Δν is the channel bandwidth and τ the integration time. Doubling the time cuts the noise by √2; a faint line that is buried at one minute can emerge after ten. This single relation is why radio observations integrate for a long time.
Practical consequenceIf your line is lost in the noise, the first thing to try is a longer observation. The second is to add spectra from a tracked run — same sky, more time.
The catch for total powerFor a broadband (band-integrated) measurement, receiver gain also drifts, adding a term that averaging cannot remove: ΔT/Tsys = √( 1/(Δντ) + (ΔG/G)² ). It is why per-channel line work is easier than absolute continuum work.

Pointing & calibration

Two corrections stand between raw hardware and trustworthy numbers: making the dish point true, and turning counts into kelvin.

Why a mount mispoints

No real mount is perfect. Its azimuth axis is slightly tilted, its zero points are offset, the structure flexes under gravity, and the feed is not perfectly centred. Left uncorrected these add up to a degree or more — a large fraction of the beam. A pointing model describes these errors with a handful of terms (axis tilts, index offsets, collimation) fitted from Sun scans, and the controller applies it to every command so the beam lands where you asked.

Judge a model by predictionA pointing model is good if it predicts where a source will be on a day it was not fitted to — not because any single term looks reasonable. This telescope's model predicts blind to about 0.03°, a small fraction of the beam.

From counts to kelvin

A raw spectrum is in arbitrary receiver counts. Two measurements fix the scale: the gain G (counts per kelvin) and the system temperature Tsys, both from a Sun scan against the known solar flux. Then

TA = (counts) / G − Tsys

There is also a fixed bandpass shape — the receiver is more sensitive at some frequencies than others — which is measured and divided out so a flat sky looks flat. With pointing, bandpass and gain all in hand, a spectrum becomes a real map of hydrogen temperature versus velocity.

An external checkPut through this chain, the telescope measured the Sun at 112 solar flux units against a reference network's 88–108 that day — agreement within the network's own spread, and an end-to-end test of the whole calibration.

The hyperfine transition

The 21 cm line is one precise change in a hydrogen atom: the electron's spin flips relative to the proton's. This is where the number comes from, and why the line is bright enough to see.

Two levels, one photon

Neutral hydrogen in its ground state has two hyperfine levels, split by whether the proton and electron spins are parallel (F=1) or antiparallel (F=0):

ν0 = 1420.405751768 MHz,   λ0 = 21.106 cm

The transition is magnetic-dipole and strongly forbidden. Its Einstein coefficient is

A10 = 2.869×10−15 s−1

so a single atom radiates about once every 11 million years. We see the line only because the Galaxy holds of order 1010 M⊙ of neutral hydrogen, and because collisions keep the two levels near thermal equilibrium at a spin temperature Ts.

Why it measures amount, not temperature

The level populations follow

n1/n0 = 3 exp(−hν0/kTs)
Since hν0/k = 0.068 K is far below any astrophysical temperature, the exponential is ≈1 and the ratio is 3 to a part in a thousand.

Three quarters of all H I atoms sit in the upper state at any moment, whatever the temperature. That is why 21 cm emission measures how much hydrogen lies along the line of sight, almost independently of how hot it is — the property that makes it such a clean tracer of galactic structure.

Column density & optical depth

The brightness temperature seen along a line of sight is set by the spin temperature and the optical depth τν:

TB = Ts ( 1 − e−τν )

For the optically thin case (τν ≪ 1), which holds for most directions away from the inner plane, this becomes TB ≈ Tsτν and the integral over the line profile gives the column density directly:

NH I = 1.823×1018 ∫ TB dv   [cm−2, TB in K, v in km/s]

This is the single most useful result in 21 cm astronomy, and it is why spectra must be stored in linear power and calibrated in kelvin, never in decibels — the integral of a logarithm means nothing.

The profile it records is the superposition of everything along the sight line, separated only in velocity, which is exactly what the tangent-point method exploits.

Galactic rotation & the tangent-point method

Because the line is intrinsically narrow, its observed frequency encodes the gas's radial velocity, and galactic rotation turns that velocity into a position. For gas in circular rotation in the plane at galactocentric radius R, seen at longitude l:

vLSR = R0 sin l [ V(R)/R − V0/R0 ]
R0 ≈ 8.2 kpc is the Sun's galactocentric distance and V0 ≈ 235 km/s the local circular speed.

The tangent point

In the first quadrant (0° < l < 90°) the line of sight reaches a minimum radius — the tangent point, at R = R0 sin l — where the whole circular velocity lies along the sight line. That gas gives the extreme (terminal) velocity in the profile, and the equation collapses to

V(R0 sin l) = vmax(l) + V0 sin l

Measuring the terminal velocity as a function of longitude traces the rotation curve directly — no distance ladder, no assumption beyond circular rotation. This is the classic experiment for a telescope this size, and everything else in the system exists to make it possible: the pointing must be good to a fraction of the beam so l means what it says, the clock must be right, and the velocity axis must be corrected to the LSR or the curve acquires an annual wobble of up to 30 km/s.

Why the channels are so fineThe 2.4 MHz band spans ±253 km/s, comfortably over the ±150 km/s galactic rotation produces. The 586 Hz channels are 0.124 km/s — some 80× finer than a typical 10 km/s line component. That is deliberate: channels can be averaged after the fact but never un-averaged, and smoothing to 10 km/s buys a factor of nine in sensitivity.

The antenna theorem & sensitivity

The dish's geometric area is Ag = πD²/4 = 7.07 m². What it actually collects — the effective area — follows from the beam through the antenna theorem AeΩA = λ²:

Ae = λ²ηMB / (1.133 θFWHM²) = 3.74 m²
with main-beam efficiency ηMB ≈ 0.7. That is an aperture efficiency ηA = Ae/Ag = 0.53, reasonable for a prime-focus dish with a simple feed.

The sensitivity in kelvin per jansky, and its reciprocal the system-equivalent flux density, are then

Γ = Ae/2k = 1.35 mK Jy−1,    SEFD = 2kTsys/Ae ≈ 5.2×104 Jy (at Tsys=70 K)

Fifty thousand janskys is a large number, and it says plainly that this is a small telescope: only a handful of the brightest radio sources are detectable in continuum at all.

System temperature & radiometry

The noise budget

The telescope always measures the source signal on top of the receiver's own noise, the system temperature. The blank-sky budget is about 70 K:

  • Low-noise amplifier — 43 K. From the SAWbird+ H1 noise figure F≈0.6 dB: Trx = T0(10F/10−1) = 290(100.06−1) = 43 K.
  • Sky — 10 K (CMB, atmosphere, galactic background).
  • Spillover & feed losses — 17 K (ground pickup past the dish edge).

The radiometer equation

The RMS fluctuation in one channel of width Δν after integrating for τ is

ΔT = ( Tsys + TA ) / √( npol Δν τ )
The source appears in the numerator because it is itself noise: a channel on a bright line fluctuates more than one beside it. For this single-polarisation receiver npol=1.

Worked example, at 586 Hz channels and Tsys=200 K: ΔT = 200/√(586×60) = 1.1 K per channel in one minute. Low-latitude H I reaches tens of kelvin, so it shows in a single minute; a few-kelvin high-latitude profile needs tens of minutes — and this equation is how you plan that.

Total-power instabilityThe ideal above misses gain drift. The practical form is ΔT/Tsys = √( 1/(Δντ) + (ΔG/G)² ), and the measured gain wobble here is ΔG/G = 2.3×10−4 (0.079 K per 60 s record). It bites only for band-integrated total power, where the thermal term divides by the channel count but the gain term does not — giving an Allan minimum for band power of about 5 s. Per-channel line work stays thermal.

The noise diode & Y-factor

A switchable noise diode of known excess temperature Tcal gives the classic Y-factor calibration (recorded now, applied in a future upgrade):

Y = Pon/Poff,   Tsys = Tcal/(Y−1),   TA = Tsys(Psource/Poff − 1)

Because the diode fires ahead of the amplifier, this tracks gain drift through an observation, which a blank-sky calibration cannot.

The calibration chain

Why only the Sun and Moon can calibrate a 5° beam

A source list with Cassiopeia A, Cygnus A and Taurus A looks like several calibrators. It is not. The beam collects 27 deg², and all three lie near the galactic plane where the diffuse continuum is 0.6–0.8× the source itself. Pointing at them measures the background as much as the source, and the part that does not cancel (the spread over off-positions a beamwidth away) runs from 14% for Taurus A to 190% for Cygnus A. The distinguishing property is not brightness but whether the background can be removed:

  • The Sun escapes by brightness: 677 K against <5 K background anywhere on the ecliptic — contamination below a per cent, no subtraction needed.
  • The Moon escapes by motion: it moves ~13°/day, so the same celestial coordinates can be observed with the Moon present and again once it has left, and the background cancels identically. It is also a black body of known size at ~225 K, so its flux is predictable.

Bandpass, then gain

The calibration in force is fitted in two steps. First a bandpass template: the dish points at the emptiest hydrogen field in the north (the Lockman Hole, l=150, b=+52), and a polynomial is fitted to the mean spectrum with the H I line masked — the instrument's spectral shape. Dividing a recording by it flattens the band without moving its level. Then the gain and system temperature, from a straight-line fit of a plane field against the survey model:

counts(ν) = G ( Tsys + TAmodel(ν) )
G is the slope, Tsys the intercept over slope. The last fit in force: G = 1.197×10−5 counts K−1, Tsys = 347 K, correlation 0.998.
Two honest caveatsThis calibrates H I against an H I model, which is circular for line science and separates G from Tsys only through the line's contrast. And it assumes the beam width, which is the largest silent systematic in the temperature scale (moving the assumed beam from 4.0° to 5.16° shifts the fitted gain 6% and Tsys 22 K). A pseudo-random-comb scheme that needs no sky model is the planned way past both.

Drift scans, and an external check

A drift scan is fitted differently — total power against the simulator's predicted curve, counts = G(Tsys + T̄model(t)) — over the continuum band only, needing no bandpass template. Put through the whole chain, the telescope measured the Sun at 112 solar flux units against a reference network's 88–108 that day: agreement within the network's own spread, and the first end-to-end test of bandpass, gain, Tsys, beam and the antenna theorem against an external standard.

Two frames & the pointing model

Everything the telescope does lives in one of two coordinate frames, and the boundary between them is where the pointing model and the velocity correction are applied.

True vs drive

True alt/az is where the dish looks on the sky, computed with the real site position. Drive is what the mount mechanically is — encoder pulses quantised to 0.5°. trueToDrive() converts one way and driveToTrue() inverts it. Every goto and track goes true→drive; every measured position (status RA/Dec, galactic l,b, Stellarium) goes drive→true.

The seven pointing terms

The correction added to a true position to get the drive position (altitude h, azimuth A) is the standard alt-az model:

Δh = IE + AN cosA + AE sinA − TF cosh
ΔA = IA + (AN sinA − AE cosA) tanh + CA sech + NPAE tanh
IE, IA encoder zero-point errors; AN, AE azimuth-axis tilt (north, east); CA collimation (beam not perpendicular to the altitude axis); NPAE axis non-perpendicularity; TF tube flexure. All seven slots always exist and default to zero; the four-term fit simply leaves CA, NPAE and TF at zero.

Refraction is applied first, at the apparent position: happ = htrue + R(htrue), then the model is evaluated there. The inverse is done by fixed-point iteration on the forward transform itself (three passes), so the two directions cannot drift apart — near the zenith a first-order inverse would be wrong by ~0.25°, half the encoder quantum.

Judge a model by predictionA pointing model is trustworthy if it predicts where a source will be on a day it was not fitted to — not because any single term looks reasonable. This mount's model predicts blind to about 0.03°. Two clamps guard the mount: the altitude fed to tan/sec is limited to 89°, and the total correction per axis to ±10° (the whole real error is ~2°).

Velocity frames

The recorded axis is topocentric — sky frequency exactly as observed, so a file re-reduces correctly whatever is later learned about the direction. The plots draw an LSR axis:

vLSR = vtopo + vbary + U·n̂
vbary is the observer's barycentric velocity toward the source; U is the solar motion (20.0 km/s toward the LSR apex). The terms are: Earth rotation 0.46, Earth orbit 29.8, solar motion 20.0 — combined ~50 km/s (≡237 kHz), so uncorrected spectra of the same source in different seasons do not superpose. Evaluated at the observation's own mid-time; the B210 clock adds a further −2.36 ppm (±0.08 km/s).

Glossary

21 cm line / H I line
The 1420.405751 MHz radio line emitted when neutral hydrogen flips its spin state. “H I” means neutral atomic hydrogen.
Alt–azimuth mount
A mount that points by altitude (angle above the horizon) and azimuth (compass bearing), rather than by celestial axes.
Antenna temperature (TA)
The power a source contributes, expressed as the temperature of a resistor that would deliver the same power. The radio unit of signal strength.
Bandpass
The receiver's built-in sensitivity-versus-frequency shape, measured and divided out so a flat sky reads flat.
Beam / FWHM
The cone of sky the dish is sensitive to. Its full width at half maximum (~5° here) sets the angular resolution.
Column density (NH)
The number of hydrogen atoms in a column of one cm² cross-section along the line of sight; proportional to the line's strength when optically thin.
Drift scan
An observation with the mount parked, letting the sky drift through the beam. Successive spectra look at different sky.
Gain (G)
The conversion factor from raw receiver counts to kelvin, found by calibration.
LSR
Local Standard of Rest: the average motion of nearby stars, used as a steady reference frame for velocities so measurements are comparable across time and direction.
Optically thin
A gas transparent to its own radiation, so the observed brightness adds up all the emitting atoms along the path.
Pointing model
A fitted description of a mount's pointing errors (tilts, offsets, flexure) that the controller applies to make the beam land on target.
Radiometer equation
ΔT = Tsys/√(Δντ): how measurement noise falls with bandwidth and integration time.
Rotation curve
Orbital speed of galactic material versus distance from the centre. Its flatness is evidence for dark matter.
SFU
Solar flux unit, 104 jansky (10−22 W m−2 Hz−1); the customary unit for the Sun's radio brightness.
System temperature (Tsys)
The receiver's own noise, expressed as a temperature. Everything is measured on top of it.
Tangent point
The place along a galactic sight line where gas moves exactly along the line of sight, giving the maximum radial velocity — the basis for measuring the rotation curve.
Topocentric
Measured from the moving telescope, before correcting for the Earth's and Sun's motion. The raw velocity frame of a recording.
Tracked observation
An observation where the mount follows the source, so every spectrum looks at the same sky.
